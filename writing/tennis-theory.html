Tennis Theory

experience isn't everything

From elementary school, my sisters and I would attend summer tennis camps and casually play on our school teams. From 12 until 19, I played on and off during summers. In college, I told my friend Alek I had been playing since I was 12, and he quickly invited me to our school's rec club.

I happily joined that evening for what seemed like a casual game of king of the court. Simple 1v1, where the king has to lose two points consecutively to be dethroned.

While we all lined up to challenge the king, I met Alek's friend Julien, who was waiting in line ahead of me. Julien said he had started playing just a year ago during COVID. I thought it was nice that the club had beginners too.

But then Julien took the court. He won the two points with two drop shots without moving from the baseline and casually took the king's place.

Julien fed me the ball and then proceeded to add backspin so that I sent my neon ball flying over the gate. I was in a state of shock: Wtf is this, how is this rec tennis? Julien had to be trolling about his single year of experience.

If he was telling the truth, starting a skill seven years earlier than Julien meant I should be at a comparable level at the very least.

But no. Julien was telling the truth, and I was getting absolutely cooked by a student who had only recently picked up tennis. The humiliation of playing against Julien became a ritual when I joined the club.

Julien's Fixation

After practice, I would join some friends in the group for late-night snacks at JustBurgers. I strategically used this time to interrogate Julien about how he actually learned. He said it was just YouTube tutorials and a friend he had played with every day during COVID for the past year. He said he really loved tennis and had suddenly lost 15 pounds from playing so regularly.

It struck me that I had never reached this level of intensity in my practice. While I started at a younger age, tennis was a summer pastime with friends and far more seasonal than Julien's one year of daily practice.

I realized there were two features to Julien's incredible leap to being a tennis prodigy:

  1. Fixation: Julien loved tennis and was able to concentrate his efforts on it over a one-year period with incredible consistency.
  2. Mentorship: Julien recognized that his form needed practice and found YouTube videos and friends who would give him the feedback he needed to actually improve on his weaknesses over that year.

I lacked consistency and form.

Luckily, these are both addressed by the universal quality of humility.

  1. Humility allows you to suck at something enough to ask for help. Find a person to admire and behavior-clone from.
  2. Humility also allows you to know that it's okay if you suck at it for A WHILE.
    • That second part is particularly important because your short-term expectations for improvement need to be dampened so that you are not massively disappointed by an initial lack of progress.

So I implemented these two things by heading to practice three evenings a week for a year and forcing Julien to play after practice occasionally. I learned a proper Western grip and found my stride.

And you know what happened?! I got good at recognizing I was bad for a few months, then got better, then suddenly started winning some points in games. Soon enough, I was helping others in tennis.

Universal Teachings of Learning

How I learned tennis carried over into almost every skill I've picked up since. Rather than believing that my classmate who had been coding since eight years old was inherently better, I remembered tennis theory and believed I could become a strong programmer with diligence and concentrated effort. When I started working on marketing on TikTok, I reminded myself that the awkward and unrewarding weeks of work would eventually hit a spike like what I felt in tennis. Moreover, I found mentors or accounts I liked to learn strategies and habits from.

I think tennis theory reminds me that learning is an awkward and nonlinear process. The formula for learning is simple: humble fixation and mentorship.

My friends occasionally glaze me for the random skills I have acquired in the last four years (cooking, marketing, programming, machine learning, research, phonology, and tennis), but I think it largely comes down to tennis theory.

A universal science to learning.
